Why I wanted to understand this

If most people use ChatGPT for free, who is paying the bill?

I type a question, I get an answer in a few seconds, and nobody charges me. That's the experience for most of the 900 million people who use ChatGPT every week. Only about 50 million of them pay.

Meanwhile, four companies alone plan to spend $725 billion on AI infrastructure this year. Somewhere between my free answer and that number there's a business. I want to follow one prompt through the whole system and see who does the work, who carries the cost, and who gets paid.

Chapter 1

Where does my prompt actually go?

Follow one sentence from your phone to a power line, and the money back the other way.

When I press enter, what happens first?

The app cuts your sentence into tokens, small chunks of words. Tokens are what the model reads and writes, and they're what everyone in this chain ends up pricing.

Does OpenAI own the computers that answer me?

Mostly not. It rents them from cloud providers who build the datacenters. Your prompt joins a batch with many other people's prompts, and the batch runs on GPUs together. That's how one expensive chip serves lots of users at once.

So what does one answer cost in electricity?

Sam Altman put the average query at about 0.34 watt-hours, about what an oven uses in a little over a second. Keep that number. It turns out to be the least important cost in the chain.

Chapter 2

If electricity is that cheap, what's expensive?

The electricity bill for one prompt

  1. An average ChatGPT query uses about 0.34 Wh (Altman, June 2025).
  2. Assume power at $0.08 / kWh, roughly a US industrial rate.
  3. 0.00034 kWh × $0.08 = $0.000027 per prompt.
  4. A billion prompts would cost about $27,000 in electricity.

So power isn't the bill. The bill is the machines and buildings that have to exist before the first prompt arrives, and they get paid for whether you use them or not.

If power is cheap per prompt, why does everyone talk about gigawatts?

Because it adds up at scale, and because the grid is slow to build. OpenAI's CFO now describes the company's capacity in power, not chips: about 0.2 GW in 2023, 0.6 GW in 2024 and roughly 1.9 GW in 2025. When a company measures itself in gigawatts, the binding constraint is how fast it can plug in more machines.

So the cost per prompt is small, and the cost of being able to answer a prompt is enormous. That's the same shape as a railway or a telecom network: a huge fixed cost spread over a very large number of tiny uses.

Chapter 3

Who's making money right now?

So who is actually profitable in this chain?

Today, the clearest answer is the chip layer. In the quarter that ended in January 2026, Nvidia kept 75 cents of gross profit on every dollar of sales, and its data center business brought in $193.7 billion over the year.

Everyone above that layer is spending ahead of revenue. The four biggest cloud companies plan to spend about $725 billion this year, up 77% from 2025. OpenAI, the biggest model company, was running at about $25 billion a year in revenue in February. The spending is roughly 29 times that revenue.

These numbers cover different 12-month windows and different companies, so read them as proportions, not as a ledger.

Chapter 4

Is this a bubble or a lag?

Bubble

  • Four companies plan $725B of AI capex in 2026 against OpenAI's $25B revenue run-rate.
  • OpenAI has reportedly lined up about $600B of compute spending through 2030.
  • The one layer clearly making money sells to everyone else at a 75% gross margin, and that margin is a cost for the rest of the stack.

Lag

  • OpenAI's revenue went from about $2B (2023) to $6B (2024) to $20B+ (2025).
  • Its compute grew about 9.5× over the same period, and revenue kept pace, per its CFO.
  • 50M people pay for ChatGPT about three years after it launched.
Where I've landed for nowUsage is real and so is the bill. The bet is whether the cost of each token falls faster than demand grows. If it does, today's capex looks early. If it doesn't, it looks like overbuilding.

Chapter 5

If I were 25, where would I build?

Token cutters

The idea
Route each request to the cheapest model that can answer it well.
Why now
GPU time is the expensive part, so every token saved is margin for your customer.
What could kill it
Model makers building the same routing in.

Price per outcome

The idea
A vertical AI product priced per result: a claim filed, a ticket closed.
Why now
Selling per seat doesn't fit software that does the work itself.
What could kill it
Outcomes that are hard to measure or verify.

Power for compute

The idea
Siting, storage and on-site power for datacenters.
Why now
Capacity is now measured in gigawatts, and the grid is the slowest thing to build.
What could kill it
Long permits and heavy capital needs.

The lessons I've written down

  1. The electricity for one prompt is tiny. The real cost is the capital that has to exist before you ask.
  2. Most users don't pay. About 1 in 18 weekly ChatGPT users does.
  3. Today the margin sits with the chip layer, at roughly 75 cents per dollar.
  4. Capex is running far ahead of model revenue: about $725B against $25B.
  5. The unit of AI capacity is now the gigawatt, so power is the bottleneck to watch.
  6. If you're building, sit where costs fall for you, or where usage is already guaranteed.
